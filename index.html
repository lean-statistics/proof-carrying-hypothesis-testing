Abstract

Progress in formalisation technology, such as Lean, has allowed advanced mathematical proofs to be confirmed mechanically by computers. As well as providing greater assurance around existing, human, proofs, an application of this technology is to validate mathematical proofs by AI. Many other uses of AI, including in medicine, justice, policy and scientific research, require comparable trust mechanisms to exist for statistical inference. To examine the feasibility of such mechanisms, we analyse several paradoxes and tensions which could make statistical inference appear difficult to certify. In answer to these challenges, we propose a protocol, proof-carrying hypothesis testing, in which an AI produces a test, with formal proof of validity, against a null hypothesis and assumption class posted by the scientist, who withholds the data. In pre-registered experiments, on data published after training cutoffs, without human assistance, now-standard AI models are shown to produce problem-specific hypothesis tests, machine-checked for validity, about coefficients or contrasts in model-robust linear regression, with strong or overwhelming evidence against published null hypotheses. We release a Lean formalisation of the finite-sample and asymptotic theory of the model-robust linear model, statistical paradoxes, and all proofs constructed through the protocol.

1 Introduction

The central question motivating this work is: “how can we trust what an AI has determined to be true based on data?”. This question is presumably on the mind of any person contemplating the use of AI in medical guidance and decisions, criminal investigation and justice, social and economic policy, or scientific research. This vast problem of trust poses challenges in data governance, AI alignment, epistemology, interpretation and specification, and statistical validity. Our paper addresses the last of these, validity, in hypothesis testing under the linear model and, within this focused setting, considers broad questions affecting statistical practice across science, medicine and economics.

Substantial progress in formalisation technology, due in particular to an enormous community effort to build up mathematical theory in Lean, has allowed systematic verification of mathematical proofs by AI. The recent formalisation of Fermat’s Last Theorem [7], and the resolution of Navier-Stokes existence and smoothness, a Millennium Prize problem [82], indicate that the capabilities of both technologies — formalisation and AI — now extend to some of the hardest mathematical problems.

Possibly dwarfing these efforts is the multilateral investment in AI for science, encompassing national and international programmes [110; 28; 30; 75; 72], dedicated science initiatives at each of the major AI laboratories [76; 6; 107; 38], startups building autonomous laboratories [32; 34]. Across these programmes, there is a hope in particular that AI could substantially improve analysis of large-scale data [109; 28], and that advances in formalisation could certify results [23; 5; 26].

Many statisticians would view certifying a statistical analysis as futile or ill-defined, because of the high degree of ambiguity, judgement and compromise involved in any practical analysis. To better define the objective, it may be productive to analyse what proof verification actually entails. Lean’s logic is a dependent type theory [70; 21; 22; 25], in which a proposition is a type, and a proof is a term of this type. Certifying a proof is checking that the constructed term is of the declared type. Validity is not universal, but relative to the system, and by Gödel’s incompleteness theorems [37] the system can never prove itself to be consistent. What has emerged is a product of many iterations deliberately circumventing identified paradoxes (Russell’s [91], Girard’s [52]).

In the same way, to develop trust mechanisms for statistical analysis, it may be best to abandon any notion of universal validity, and to start instead from the existing tensions and paradoxes in the field: build a system which, at least, has answers to those known problems. There is already a substantial literature in Statistics on the foundations of inference, which has been shaped by the discovery of paradoxes and impossibility theorems, including Hume’s problem of induction [51], the Bahadur–Savage theorem [10], Lindley’s paradox [63], Simpson’s paradox [100], and more. Other tensions which have influenced the field include the notion that ‘all models are wrong’ [16], motivating model-robust theory [17], the use of asymptotic statements for finite sample inference, and the reproducibility crisis [81], motivating e-values [96; 39]. Despite this comprehensive theory there is no computational system in which these tensions and paradoxes are satisfactorily addressed. We think many readers will have implicitly recognised that a mathematically-capable AI may essentially be necessary for such a system to work in practice.

In this paper, we present a protocol for hypothesis testing using AI. The central novelty is proof carrying: the AI supplies a proof of the validity of its proposal relative to the specific hypothesis testing problem given. We build the protocol in a way which directly confronts major statistical paradoxes and tensions, mirroring, in a limited way, how modern formalisation technology itself is designed as an answer to logical paradoxes. We focus on the problem of testing a parameter under the linear model.

2 Related work

The key novelty of our proposal is that an AI develops and formalises a mathematical statement within the statistical protocol and we explain below why this difference over existing proposals is crucial.

There are many existing proposals for using AI for statistical inference on data. These include: agents that choose hypotheses and report p-values, calibration to e-values before combining, validity of each p-value assumed [48]; a human-proved, machine-checked multiple-testing theorem wrapped around LLM-generated analyses, with each test called by a ‘harness’ [93]; an agent that proposes candidates while a frozen statistical referee judges them by betting [86]; e-processes computed by a fixed procedure over LLM outputs [92; 20]; betting strategies learned from the data as they arrive, within a function class [85; 97]; and agents that report findings with no statistical guarantee [73; 66].

Several other systems aim to certify statistical inference on data more generally. StatWhy and Why3-py verify that a program applies a named hypothesis test in accordance with annotated preconditions, such as the data being normally distributed [56; 104]. Tacet, targeting machine-learning leaderboards, is a formalised multiple testing accounting layer over given p-values [3]. Tea and Tisane choose a test on the analyst’s behalf, by checking the preconditions of a fixed collection of classical tests against the data and substituting a rank-based test where a parametric approach is not supported [54; 55]. The software accompanying the e-value and anytime-valid literature provides sequential tests and confidence sequences whose exact validity is established under different conditions [67; 47; 64]. Tools such as statcheck recompute reported p-values from the accompanying test statistics and so verify the arithmetic of a published analysis, not its assumptions [80]. More widely there are processes in science which aim to improve reproducibility of studies, such as replication studies [81], reporting guidelines [94], and the pre-registration of hypotheses [79], which is highly compatible with our proposal.

In all the above, where there is a theorem (formalised or not) proving validity, it is derived once, before any specific statistical problem is given. This means that the mathematical analysis is not specialised to the problem. A scientist cannot say, “my errors are a mixture of two normals with unknown weights” and expect this assumption to have been anticipated by the theory. This is why our protocol allows the AI to propose the test and prove its validity after the scientist has presented the problem; and why a mathematically capable AI is essentially required for the idea to work in practice: every hypothesis test is a possibly unique mathematical problem.

The protocol recommends e-values, a measure of evidence which can be connected to betting. Our only novelty claim around this concept is its justification through Lindley’s paradox, which is mathematically elementary but fundamental to our proposal. For other results and ideas surrounding e-values, see e.g. Shafer [96]; Vovk and Wang [112]; Grünwald et al. [39]; Ramdas et al. [87]; Ramdas and Wang [88] and further references.

The protocol also recommends model-robust linear regression, which we have implemented in Lean following White [116]; MacKinnon and White [68]; Fahrmeir [31]; Abadie et al. [2]; Kuchibhotla et al. [59, 58]. In the course of running the protocol in real data examples, several theorems were derived by AI, most of them instances of known results. We contrast these against existing results [43; 11; 24; 12; 84; 45; 46; 29; 59; 2] in Section 5.1.

The impetus for this project was news, in the Lean community forum, of the development of the Central Limit Theorem (CLT), eventually merged into the core mathematical library Mathlib in March 2026 [69]. There are several broader initiatives to formalise statistics, including Statlib [102], StatsMLlib [103; 120; 101] and an asymptotic theory formalised by LLM agents [114], as well as individual results [27; 8; 106; 9]. The focus of the present paper is less formalisation of theory, than the use of formalisation to certify statistical inference by AI.

The proposal is based on Lean [25] and its mathematical library Mathlib (Lean 4) [108], and is premised on the availability of an AI system which can both derive and formalise mathematics. Several models now exist to do this [50; 89; 62; 113; 4; 7].

3 The proof-carrying hypothesis testing protocol

Our proposed protocol is described in Box 1, and a practical walkthrough is given in Appendix G. In this paper it is implemented for the linear model, with a design (or “feature”) matrix 𝐗\mathbf{X}, responses Y1,…,YnY_{1},\dots,Y_{n}, parameter β∗\beta^{*} and null hypothesis H0:a⊤⁢β∗=vH_{0}:a^{\top}\beta^{*}=v, all detailed in Section 4. The protocol comprises three parties: a scientist, an AI, and a proof checker, which we will take to be the Lean kernel. The scientist provides the fixed inputs (here, a numeric design matrix), the null hypothesis and the assumption class (e.g. independence, and numeric error and standard deviation bounds). The observed values y1,…,yny_{1},\dots,y_{n} are withheld. The AI returns an e-variable, a non-negative function ee of nn real arguments, together with a Lean proof that 𝔼F⁢[e⁢(Y1,…,Yn)]≤1\mathbb{E}_{F}[e(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n})]\leq 1 for every distribution FF satisfying 𝒜\mathcal{A} and H0H_{0} (written F∈𝒜∩H0F\in\mathcal{A}\cap H_{0} hereafter); in constructing ee the AI may use the fixed inputs, the hypothesis, the assumption class, domain knowledge, and any mathematics it can prove, but not the data. The kernel checks the proof against the scientist’s posting. The data are then entered and the number e⁢(y1,…,yn)e(y_{1},\dots,y_{n}) is output, an e-value for H0H_{0}.

The proof in the Propose step ensures that e⁢(y1,…,yn)e(y_{1},\dots,y_{n}) is a realisation of a valid e-variable: a non-negative function E=e⁢(Y1,…,Yn)E=e(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}) of the data whose expectation is at most one under every distribution FF satisfying 𝒜\mathcal{A} and H0H_{0} [112]. A high e-value is regarded as intrinsically strong evidence against H0H_{0}. If desired, it can be converted into a valid p-value via p=1/ep=1/e, as by Markov’s inequality ℙF⁢(1/E≤α)≤α\mathbb{P}_{F}(1/E\leq\alpha)\leq\alpha for any α∈[0,1]\alpha\in[0,1] for any distribution F∈𝒜∩H0F\in\mathcal{A}\cap H_{0}.

The protocol in Box 1 constructs an e-value, which we highlight is a choice: the same protocol can construct instead a p-value P=p⁢(Y1,…,Yn)P=p(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}) with the guarantee ℙF⁢(P≤α)≤α\mathbb{P}_{F}(P\leq\alpha)\leq\alpha for any α∈[0,1]\alpha\in[0,1] and any distribution F∈𝒜∩H0F\in\mathcal{A}\cap H_{0}. Our preference for e-values is explained in Section 6.

What distinguishes the protocol from a statistical package is that the mathematics is not fixed in advance. Each posting defines a mathematical problem, and the AI’s e-variable is a theorem (𝔼⁢{e⁢(Y1,…,Yn)}≤1\mathbb{E}\{e(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n})\}\leq 1 for all F∈𝒜∩H0F\in\mathcal{A}\cap H_{0}) and a proof. As an example of why the AI might adapt its strategy to the design, certain AI-derived bounds in Section 1 can be inapplicable when the leverage is too large.

We have named this protocol by analogy to proof-carrying code [77], a mechanism in which an “untrusted code producer must supply with the code a safety proof that attests to the code’s adherence to a previously defined safety policy”.

3.1 Risks

This protocol assumes trust in the scientist neither to set assumptions to reach desired conclusions, nor to select the null hypothesis on the basis of the data; these issues are addressed by additional protocols such as pre-registration.

Beyond the above, we perceive the main risks of this protocol to be as follows. The protocol can only be run once on a dataset, which means the assumption class has to be final. If it is too large, evidence will not be found, and if it is too small, the evidence is unreliable.

In practice, the scientist may simulate data under a specified alternative hypothesis H1H_{1} with distribution F1F_{1}, using the AI’s proposal, to evaluate the power of the proposal, i.e. ℙF1⁢(E≥1/α)\mathbb{P}_{F_{1}}(E\geq 1/\alpha), before applying it to the data. The proposal can then be improved, e.g. using a different AI model, without affecting validity, but the scientist should be wary of divulging properties of the data through these iterations (the design can be revealed).

The protocol holds for a proposal generated independently from YY, which may be hard to guarantee for historical data, some derivative of which the AI may have seen in training. However, the proposals are not ‘black-boxes’, they are mathematical statements which can be checked for suspicious targeting. The power of the protocol is a function of the capability of the AI, an inferior model is limited by what it can prove and formalise; also, nothing in the protocol obliges the AI to construct a strong proposal. The posting step in the protocol has a two-level “specification risk”: the real problem being correctly transcribed into a mathematical hypothesis test; and then the latter being correctly transcribed into Lean. Finally, it may be debatable what is ‘model’ versus ‘assumption’: an important design choice in our implementation of the linear model was to define the model and projection parameter without assuming independence (equation (1), formalised without independence), so that this assumption is clearly signposted in 𝒜\mathcal{A}. We discuss potential extensions of the protocol to allow multiple proposals and optional continuation in the supplementary material.

4 Model-robust regression

Linear regression refers to a collection of statistical tools, designed to understand the mechanism by which variables predict an outcome, built around the characteristic equation

Y=𝐗⁢β+e,Y=\mathbf{X}\beta+e,

where Y∈ℝnY\in\mathbb{R}^{n} represents a collection of outcomes, 𝐗∈ℝn×p\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p} holds the values of the corresponding predictor variables, e∈ℝne\in\mathbb{R}^{n} represents the prediction errors, and β∈ℝp\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{p} is a vector of unknown coefficients to be estimated. We consider the ordinary least squares (OLS) estimate,

β^=arg⁢minβ∈ℝp⁡‖Y−𝐗⁢β‖2=(𝐗⊤⁢𝐗)−1⁢𝐗⊤⁢Y,\hat{\beta}=\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\|Y-\mathbf{X}% \beta\|^{2}=(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\top}Y,

when the design matrix 𝐗\mathbf{X} is deterministic, and the outcomes in YY are random.

We treat the ‘misspecified’ case in which the mean of YY is not necessarily 𝐗⁢β\mathbf{X}\beta for some β\beta, i.e., the true underlying relationship between outcome and predictor variables might be nonlinear. This general setting is known in the literature as assumption-lean [13; 111] or model-robust [116], although within this setting treating 𝐗\mathbf{X} as deterministic is perhaps atypical [17; 60]. We have opted for this treatment to align with mainstream use cases, e.g. two-group testing or trend estimation, where this is often the more realistic regime.

In the model-robust setting, β^\hat{\beta} estimates an optimal linear approximation,

β⋆=arg⁢minβ∈ℝp⁡𝔼⁢‖Y−𝐗⁢β‖2=(𝐗⊤⁢𝐗)−1⁢𝐗⊤⁢μ,μi=𝔼⁢[Yi],\beta^{\star}=\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\mathbb{E}\|Y-% \mathbf{X}\beta\|^{2}=(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mu,% \qquad\mu_{i}=\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}], (1)

which we take as the target of inference, whether or not the model is well-specified.

We consider hypothesis tests of the general form H0:a⊤⁢β∗=vH_{0}:a^{\top}\beta^{*}=v, where the contrast aa and value vv are given. For example, if β∗\beta^{*} contains two coefficients, we could pick v=0v=0, and a=(1,0)⊤a=(1,0)^{\top} or a=(1,−1)⊤a=(1,-1)^{\top} to test respectively whether the first coefficient is zero or the two coefficients are equal.

Estimation of the mean is a special case of the (misspecified) linear model p=1p=1 with 𝐗=(1,…,1)⊤\mathbf{X}=(1,\dots,1)^{\top}, in which β^\hat{\beta} is the sample mean and β⋆\beta^{\star} the average of the μi\mu_{i}. The Welch t-test for the difference in the mean outcomes of two groups, certainly the most frequently performed statistical procedure in science, also arises as a special case (Appendix C).

5.2 Data

In this section we demonstrate the protocol on 13 datasets. In each case, we imagine we were the scientist looking at the data for the first time, with a relevant null hypothesis and contextual knowledge. Our assumption class always contains independence, and assumptions are selected extremely conservatively (with an exception explained below). For example, for Galton’s data on children’s and parents’ heights (the discovery of regression to the mean), we assume errors of at most 18 inches with standard deviation at most 4.5 inches. The assumptions chosen for each dataset are given in the supplementary material.

The protocol is stated assuming one AI proposal and we discuss briefly how this could be extended to multiple agents in the supplementary material (Appendix A). In the following experiments, multiple proposals are presented simply to allow a method comparison.

The datasets were selected to cover consequential linear regressions across science, policy, medicine and AI, alongside standard pedagogical examples, with a public source for each analysis. We take a p-value to have been reported only if it is printed in some public source (sources given in the supplementary material). If an interval was printed, we select the upper bound. The datasets and assumptions-per-dataset are described in Appendix E.

The proof-carrying e-values and p-values derived by AI are shown in Figure 1, p-values on the left, and e-values on the right, on a log-scale, alongside the reported values, the Monte Carlo search, and the asymptotic p-values. The asymptotic validity of the asymptotic p-values is formalised, but these are not allowable within the protocol. An arrow indicates that the corresponding value lies beyond the plotting region and is extreme.

The exception mentioned above is the normal-approximation route and the accompanying Monte Carlo search, which are instead evaluated under stringent assumptions (equal variances, Gaussian moment ratios). This has two purposes. The first is to show that the normal-approximation route adopted (numerator/denominator split, Berry-Esseen above, Cantelli below) is not competitive. This also explains why we have calculated the bounds using the best constants available in the literature [98; 99] rather than what has currently been formalised (a constant of 3030, 119). The second is to demonstrate that several of the (extreme) asymptotic p-values cannot be supported based on any central limit theorem without some further assumption. We have conducted a search over the assumption class, and report the largest exceedance probability found, computed by Monte Carlo with precise error bounds; the class limit is at least this value.

Conclusions which can be drawn from these experiments are: the use of asymptotic p-values for any of the (finite) sample sizes encountered in our examples is not supported by a central limit theorem, unless assumptions beyond moments, such as Gaussian shape, are made; machine-checked routes report strong e-value and p-value evidence in several cases; the best performing method however was suggested to us by an expert and not AI, and indeed this expert judgement also brought material improvements to the normal-approximation route allowing us to lose a misspecification tolerance; the choice of assumption class (e.g. moment versus error bounds) has large implications for the result and must be declared, well-motivated and conservative.

The demonstration departs from the protocol in considering datasets which are likely to have either entered, or at least not to be independent from, the data used to train AI. The bounds derived are generic, which limits what can have been gained, but in general this could compromise the validity of the hypothesis test. In Appendix F, the protocol is run exactly as specified, with one proposal and no human help, on three further datasets published after AI training cut-offs.

In each of the 16 real data examples, the AI has had to develop a mathematical solution to a hypothesis testing problem for which no published test applies directly. The tests a scientist would otherwise use, such as implemented in standard statistical packages (e.g. lm in R), are exact only under Gaussian errors and correct specification.

6 Design principles

The proof-carrying protocol has three design choices, model-robust versus well-specified, assumption class versus asymptotics, e-values versus p-values, direct answers to existing tensions and paradoxes in Statistics which we now discuss.

The implementation of model-robust theory is an answer to Box’s famous “all models are wrong” [16], as β∗\beta^{*} is formally defined under only the assumption that the Y1,…,YnY_{1},\ldots,Y_{n} follow some joint distribution with finite mean. A disadvantage of this approach is that β∗\beta^{*} loses some interpretability, the risk of which is perhaps well-illustrated with Simpson’s paradox. The conclusion that boys perform better than girls, when in fact they pick easier subjects, as the hidden subject covariate is left out of the design, gets labelled as ‘robustness’ to missing covariates, rather than a modelling error [18].

The Bahadur–Savage theorem [10] (Lean statement below) motivates our preference for an explicit assumption class, over asymptotic p- or e-values. This result demonstrates that finite-sample inference about a mean (and by extension the parameters of a linear regression), is futile without assumptions. A p-value which is valid for any distribution with mean μ0\mu_{0} has no power to detect any deviation from μ0\mu_{0}.

A corresponding result holds for e-values since any valid e-value provides a valid p-value. In view of this theorem, we have failed to make asymptotic p- or e-values possible within a formal framework, each of our attempts somehow exposing an assumption either unrealistic (e.g. data exactly or almost exactly Gaussian) or providing unusable bounds (see Section 1).

Lindley’s paradox [63] (Lean statement below) motivates our preference for e-values. Given i.i.d. normal⁢(μ,σ2)\text{normal}(\mu,\sigma^{2}) data in which we are testing H0:μ=μ0H_{0}:\mu=\mu_{0}, it is possible for data to simultaneously exhibit an arbitrarily small p-value (pp) for H0H_{0} and an arbitrarily high posterior (π\pi) for H0H_{0}, even though H0H_{0} and H1H_{1} were equally likely a priori (c=1/2c=1/2 below) and μ\mu is uniformly distributed over some interval around μ0\mu_{0} under H1H_{1} ([μ0−l,μ0+l][\mu_{0}-l,\mu_{0}+l], below).

7 Conclusion

We have presented a statistical protocol, proof-carrying hypothesis testing, in which an AI produces a test and a formal proof of validity against a statistical problem set by the scientist, who withholds the data. In a demonstration on thirteen datasets, the AI derived and formalised finite-sample p-values and e-values for regression contrasts under declared error and variance bounds, finding strong evidence in several cases. At those sample sizes, asymptotic p-values based on the CLT cannot be supported without further assumptions. On three additional datasets, published after AI training cut-offs, the largest of the four AI models tested produced unattended a machine-checked test with strong or overwhelming evidence (e-values from 110 to 1.9×10441.9\times 10^{44}) for every dataset, with and without our Lean library. In general, the power of the protocol is a function of the mathematical capability of the AI.

A problem we have not solved is how asymptotic theory, central to statistical reasoning, could be used within a practical system of formal inference. Attempts to extract when ‘nn is large enough’ from an asymptotic proof may fail, for example, when a step invokes a relationship between two entire sequences, (am→0)⇒(bm→0)(a_{m}\rightarrow 0)\Rightarrow(b_{m}\rightarrow 0) so that ana_{n} being small for the specific sample size nn says nothing about bnb_{n} being small. Proof mining [57] and metastability [105] provide more sophisticated approaches for extracting finite-sample statements from asymptotic proofs, and whether these could bring acceptable guarantees on real data is an open question.

Appendix A The betting analogy

The world of betting gives a useful interpretation of the e-value [Shafer et al., 2011, Shafer, 2021, Ramdas et al., 2023, Grünwald et al., 2024]: a bookmaker is imagined to believe the null model for the data; a bet with a stake of 1 unit offers a payout e⁢(y)e(y) depending on the realised value of the data yy; this bet is acceptable to the bookmaker if (in their eyes) its expectation does not exceed 1. A large payout is evidence against H0H_{0} in the plain sense that losing a great deal of money points to a flaw in the bookmaker’s model.

The size of the assumption class can be interpreted as the bookmaker’s advantage: The larger the class, the weaker the assumptions, the less any bet can pay. The Bahadur–Savage theorem [Bahadur and Savage, 1956] (Section 6) shows a case in which the assumption class is so large than no bet can have positive expected return even when the null hypothesis is false.

The betting picture also makes it intuitive how evidence can accumulate over time and across studies without hitting the difficulties that p-values meet under multiple comparisons and optional stopping. E-values of arbitrary dependence may be combined by averaging, just as a bookmaker expects to win, on average, against multiple punters. It is perfectly valid to report the present e-value, or choose ask for more data and continue, just as a bookmaker expects to win whether the punter stops or decides to continue betting based on past outcomes. Obviously we do not view these analogies as formal guarantees, but they evoke the possibility of extending the protocol to a full “betting market” involving multiple agents, staged release of data, and continuation of studies.

Appendix G Practical guidance

This section shows what a scientist writes to run the protocol, what the AI returns, and what is checked, using the COVALENT posting of Appendix F as the example.

In what follows, whenever the scientist “writes Lean”, we mean that the scientist is responsible for the content, which must reflect their model, null hypothesis and assumptions before observing data, and this may amount to checking 1 to 15 lines of code (depending on how much is new, see below); under this strict condition, these lines can be written by an AI.

The scientist’s file is a form with four entries; the AI’s file is a proof, which the scientist is not required to read (but can).

The posting.
⬇
def posting : Posting where
design := Design.twoGroups 799 812
contrast := ![-1, 1]
nullValue := 0
assumptions := Assumptions.independentBounded
(errorBound := 1) (sdBound := 1/2)

The four entries are the design matrix; the contrast aa and the null value vv of H0:a⊤⁢β⋆=vH_{0}:a^{\top}\beta^{\star}=v; and the assumption class (here, independent errors, |Yi−𝔼⁢Yi|≤1|Y_{i}-\mathbb{E}Y_{i}|\leq 1, Var⁢Yi≤1/4\mathrm{Var}Y_{i}\leq 1/4). The designs available are Design.twoGroups, Design.doseResponse (an intercept and a dose column, as in the second posting of Appendix F) and Design.matrix, a numeric matrix of moderate size, for which the invertibility of X⊤⁢XX^{\top}X is checked by computation. Recall that by the Bahadur–Savage theorem (Section 6) there is no non-trivial exact test if the scientist cannot declare assumptions.

A different assumption class.

An assumption class is a condition on the distribution FF and the outcomes YY. The class above is defined in the library by

⬇
def independentBounded (errorBound sdBound : ℝ\mathbb{R}) : Assumptions :=
fun F Y => Independent Y F
∧\wedge (∀\forall i, |Y i - E_F[Y i]| ≤\leq errorBound)
∧\wedge (∀\forall i, Var_F[Y i] ≤\leq sdBound ^ 2)

A scientist desiring a different assumption class, for instance dropping the variance bound, writes a condition of the same form. The e-variables available in the library rely on the assumption class above; under a new assumption class, the AI must prove validity just from Mathlib and the definitions, which is more computational work.

A different model.

The library is written for the linear model: a posting is a design, a contrast and a null value, and the data are nn real outcomes. A posting for another model, a variance ratio or a test of independence, requires new Lean statements for the data, the parameter and the null hypothesis. This amounts to replacing about 15 lines of code (defining the estimator, the parameter β⋆\beta^{\star} and H0H_{0}). By contrast, AI proofs in the protocol can run to hundreds or thousands of lines of code.

Appendix I Monte Carlo search

The Monte Carlo search of Figure 1 looks for a distribution in the assumption class of the normal-approximation route under which the observed studentised contrast is as probable as possible.

The discovery of a single candidate producing a p-value much larger than the asymptotic p-value (e.g. cars: 6×10−236\times 10^{-23} versus 5×10−45\times 10^{-4}; anorexia: 7×10−47\times 10^{-4} versus 0.0110.011) demonstrates that the asymptotic p-value cannot be supported by the assumption class. The number reported is the largest probability ℙF⁢(|T|≥t0)\mathbb{P}_{F}(|T|\geq t_{0}) found for any FF in the assumption class, a lower bound on the class supremum c⁢(𝒜)=supF∈𝒜ℙF⁢(|T|≥t0)c(\mathcal{A})=\sup_{F\in\mathcal{A}}\mathbb{P}_{F}(|T|\geq t_{0}).

Each candidate is a collection of discrete distributions, one for each error, and a misspecification vector. It is confirmed to be a valid member of the assumption class by directly computing its moments, which can be done exactly because the distributions are finite and discrete, checking that they satisfy the bounds of Appendix E (smin=smaxs_{\min}=s_{\max}, b3=8/πb_{3}=\sqrt{8/\pi}, κ=3\kappa=3). The probability |T|≥t0|T|\geq t_{0} is estimated by sampling from the candidate (4×1064\times 10^{6}, 2×1062\times 10^{6} or 5×1055\times 10^{5} by nn) and the 95% lower confidence limit reported.

The search is heuristic and developed by AI: error distributions comprise 89 atoms in [−7,7][-7,7]; coordinate ascent maximising a smoothed Monte Carlo estimate of ℙF⁢(|T|≥t0)\mathbb{P}_{F}(|T|\geq t_{0}), each step replacing one error distribution by the solution of a linear programme over the grid.

We do not guarantee that the space is well-explored, and can provide only modest evidence in cases where the p-value is large: each dataset was searched four times (two starting distributions, each with and without misspecification); for anorexia, streptomycin and Card--Krueger, where the probability found is above 1%, the four searches agree within 2%; for cars and Boston within a factor of four; for GISTEMP and Reinhart--Rogoff, where the probability found is of order 10−610^{-6}, the four searches disagree, and the largest value is reported.

The code, including the check of a saved candidate, is in mc_search/ of the repository.
